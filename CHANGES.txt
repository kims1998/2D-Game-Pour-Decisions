POUR DECISIONS - CHANGES FROM THE DESIGN DOCUMENT
==================================================

The one-pager described a 2D bartending skill game: pour liquor into glasses of
different shapes and sizes and hit an exact target (half a shot, one shot, a
double) by eye. That core is intact. This file lists what changed while building it, and why.


1. NAME
-------
The game is now called "Pour Decisions".


2. HOW POURING IS SIMULATED (biggest change)
--------------------------------------------
Design: flow rate would be some function of tilt and spout, needing lots of
playtesting ("30 ml/s is realistic but brutal").

Built: flow comes from the bottle's actual shape instead of a hand-tuned curve.
  - Each bottle's interior is stored as polygons. Every frame the game finds
    the horizontal liquid surface inside the tilted bottle, then measures how
    far the spout sits below that surface (the "head").
  - Flow follows Torricelli's law (speed grows with the square root of head)
    until the spout's own restriction caps it.
  - Result: a full bottle starts pouring around 80 degrees and a nearly empty
    one around 100 to 110, and the stream tapers as the bottle drains. Nobody
    had to tune that; it falls out of the geometry.
  - Calibration ended up at 14 ml/s (slow pourer) to 48 ml/s (open neck). A
    shot takes about 3.2 s on a slow pourer and about 1 s on an open neck.
  - Headless tests (Tools/PhysicsTests) check start angles, pour times and
    grading for every bottle, plus whether the stream lands in every glass.

Liquid in flight is real now. The bottle emits small parcels of liquid that
fall under gravity, and they only count once they land in the glass. Stop
tilting and what's already in the air still lands, like a real pour. Parcels
that hit the rim or miss the glass become spills and puddles on the counter.


3. LIQUIDS
----------
Design: whiskey, vodka, tequila, rum.
Built: whiskey, vodka, tequila, dark rum, gin, coffee liqueur.
Coffee liqueur was added as a "thick" liquid.
Thick liquids pour slower, need extra tilt before they move, and respond
sluggishly, so easing off doesn't slow them right away and you have to back off
early. Once the spout rises above the liquid the stream breaks off fast. The
tests caught an early version where thick liquids kept dribbling onto the
counter after the bottle came back up, and that was fixed.


4. SPOUTS AND "THE POUR WILL NEVER BE CONSISTENT"
-------------------------------------------------
There are four spouts: Slow Pourer, Speed Pourer, Wide Pourer and Open Neck. Each order also
gets a random flow variance from its spout (plus or minus 4 to 12%), so no two
pours behave exactly alike. Open necks and nearly empty bottles "glug": flow
surges as air gulps back in, with a matching glug sound.


5. GLASSES
----------
Six glasses are in: shot, tall shooter, rocks, highball, pint and
martini. Each is modeled as a truncated cone
in real centimeters, and the art is drawn from the same numbers, so what you
see is exactly what's scored. The martini glass is a true cone, so it fills
deceptively fast at the bottom.


6. CONTROLS
-----------
Mouse: you can click and drag anywhere in the play area, not just on the bottle
itself. Grabbing a tiny bottle was fiddly, and the bottle now moves itself over
the glass as it tilts (like a bartender's wrist), so it never has to be aimed.
Drag distance still maps to tilt angle (analog). Grabbing a bottle that's
already tilted picks up where it is instead of snapping.

Keyboard "hard mode": hold Space / Right Arrow / D to tilt at a steady rate and
let go to stop (the bottle returns upright). Hard mode pays 25% more points.
A mouse sensitivity option was added.

Other keys: Enter or E to serve, Esc or P to pause, Tab to skip (Training).


7. MODES AND PROGRESSION
------------------------
  - Classic: five shifts (Opening Shift, Happy Hour, Weekend Rush, Lost the
    Spouts, Last Call). Each adds glass types, faster or messier spouts, more
    liquids, shorter timers, and dimmer lights. You need one star to unlock the
    next shift, and you can earn up to three.
  - Training (new): no clock, a target line shown in the glass, a live
    milliliter readout, and the glass's capacity on the ticket.
Progress and best scores are saved per shift.
A 90-second "Rush" mode was built and then cut, to keep the game focused on
Classic progression and Training.


8. SCORING
----------
Tolerances scale with the order size, so a half shot isn't unfairly harder than
a double:
  Perfect: within max(1.5 ml, 5%)    Great: within max(3 ml, 10%)
  Good: within max(5 ml, 18%)        otherwise Short or Over
Spilling more than a quarter of the order, or overflowing the glass, is graded
"Spilled". Small splashes cost a few points. You also get bonuses for time left
and for streaks. Tips fill a tip jar.


9. READABILITY AND FAIRNESS (the doc's concerns)
------------------------------------------------
  - Thin layers: the camera eases into a close-up on the glass while you pour,
    and liquids get a bright meniscus band at the surface, so even clear vodka
    reads clearly.
  - Fairness on tapered glasses: art and math come from the same centimeter
    values; after every serve, a dashed line shows where the target actually
    was; Training shows each glass's capacity.
  - Dim lighting: the last two Classic shifts darken the room and leave a pool
    of light over the glass. Dark rum and coffee liqueur get hard to
    see, which is what the doc asked for.


10. AUDIO
---------
All the SFX from the doc are in: pour loop, glugs, glass clink, glass slide,
splash and spill, cork pop, bottle lift, grade stingers, UI clicks and hover,
cash register, coins. Timer ticks, level complete/fail and a
whoosh were added. Two pour behaviors go beyond the doc:
  - The pour loop's volume follows how much liquid is landing (not how much is
    leaving the bottle), and its pitch rises as the glass fills, like a real
    glass. In the dim levels the sound is the best cue you have.

Music: cut. The doc planned a lounge loop for Classic, an upbeat track for
Rush and a title loop. A procedural jazz backing track was built and then
removed. The game has no background music; the sound design relies on the
effects above, especially the pour sound, which you need to hear clearly. It was
removed because the background music would play heavily over the other sounds.
So, it was removed entirely to show that the other sounds are noticeable.


11. ART AND AUDIO SOURCES
-------------------------
Design: take free asset packs from online.
Built: all sprites and sounds are generated by our own scripts in Tools/
(generate_art.py with Pillow, generate_audio.py with numpy/scipy). This was done
because the glass and bottle art has to match the physics exactly, down to the
centimeter, which downloaded packs couldn't do. It also keeps one consistent
style. The only outside assets are two Google Fonts (Lilita One and Nunito,
SIL Open Font License; license files are in Resources/Fonts).

Exception: the background and counter come from a free CC0
asset pack ("Free Bar Assets Pack" by Styloo, itch.io). Tools/compose_stock_art.py
lays its pieces (wall, beam, lamps, shelf, bottles, chalkboards, fridge, clock)
out into a full scene and stretches its counter across the screen.

The art then goes through a retro pixel pass (Tools/pixelate_art.py): each
sprite is shrunk to chunky pixels with a small palette and imported with point
filtering, for a simple 8-bit look. Sprites keep their exact sizes, so the art
still lines up with the physics.

Art made: 6 bottles (back and front layers, with a visible liquid level
inside), 3 spout attachments, 6 glasses (back and front layers), the bar
background and counter, 6 customers with 3 expressions each, order ticket, recipe card, speech bubble, tip jar,
coins, stars, icons, logo, buttons and panels, and particle sprites.


12. NOT DONE / CUT
------------------
  - Gamepad trigger support and mobile touch (listed as "possible later").
  - Mixed drinks (out of scope from the start).
  - A pour "travel delay" setting is unnecessary now that liquid in flight is
    simulated.
